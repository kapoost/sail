## About this game

**A sailing simulator where the sailing is the simulation.**

Most games put a wind arrow on the screen and a multiplier under the boat. This one
models the thing itself: apparent wind from true wind and boat speed, lift and drag from
each sail at its own angle of attack, heel against righting moment, leeway, rudder stall,
weather helm, and the hull-speed wall that a displacement boat cannot be driven through.
Trim is yours. The sheet sets the angle; the angle sets the force; the force sets the
heel; the heel takes the force away again.

**A sea built out of twenty-one wave components**, summed on the graphics card, with the
same arithmetic running under the hull — so the water you are looking at and the water
she is floating in are the same water. Fetch-limited: the sea to windward of a lee shore
is as big as the distance to that shore, which is why an archipelago is a different place
to sail than open water.

**An archipelago that is a coast, not a scatter.** The islands lie in a chain, the way
real island groups do, with an inside and an outside and channels between them. They stop
the water being deep, they stop the wind, and they stop the sea — and reading all three is
what coastal sailing is.

**Work worth doing.**

* **Deliveries** between the harbours of the group, by chart and compass.
* **Pilotage** — a loaded brig stands in from seaward wanting a pilot for the inner
  passage. She draws four metres where you draw two, she will not lie closer than
  sixty-five degrees to the wind, and she follows the water you cross. You talk to her
  with signal flags out of the International Code, and she answers with the answering
  pendant.
* **Fishing** — a rod, a drag you can set, and fish that fight.
* **Salvage** — a deck cargo went over the side in the last gale and the casks are still
  coming ashore.
* **A platform of your own**, moored in an anchorage, where everything you land is piled
  where you can see it from the water, and where a chandler will build you a derrick, a
  drying rack or a lamp for what you have collected.

**Navigation you can actually do.** Hand bearing compass, three-bearing fixes with a
cocked hat, dead reckoning, a chart you draw on with dividers and a parallel rule, a
deck log, and lights with their real characteristics. Nothing on the paper tells you
where you are until you work it out.

**Two to eight friends in one archipelago.** Every boat is computed by the person sailing
her; the server is a relay, a clock and a lobby.

**Everything is generated.** The sea, the sky, the deck planking, the compass card, the
coastline, the sound of the rigging — all of it comes out of code and a seed, which is
why the world is the same world next week and the weather never is.

## Early Access questionnaire

**Why Early Access?**

> The hard half of this game is finished and the comfortable half is not. The simulation —
> the rig, the hull, the sea, the coast — is built and tested against figures a sailor
> knows from the water, and there are four hundred and ten automated tests that say so on
> every change. What it needs now is the work that only comes from other people sailing
> it: which jobs are worth doing twice, what is missing from the chart table, where a
> passage stops being interesting. That is not a thing anybody can decide alone in a room.

**Approximately how long will this game be in Early Access?**

> Six to twelve months. The list is written down and public — tides, night, fog, weather
> that moves through a passage, gear that breaks — and the game leaves Early Access when
> that list is done rather than when a date arrives.

**How is the full version planned to differ from the Early Access version?**

> Tidal streams and a tidal window to plan a passage around. Night, with pilotage by the
> lights' own characteristics. Fog, and blind pilotage by sounder and clock. Weather that
> veers and builds through an afternoon instead of standing still. Gear that fails — a
> parted sheet, a jammed furler, a leak. Anchoring and mooring as the end of a passage.
> A second boat with a different feel. And a career that ties the jobs together.

**What is the current state of the Early Access version?**

> Playable and complete as far as it goes. You can sail, navigate, make deliveries, pilot
> a square-rigger in, fish, collect salvage, build on your platform, and sail with other
> people. There is a fifteen-step lesson aboard for anybody who has never sailed. What is
> missing is listed above, not hidden.

**Will the game be priced differently during and after Early Access?**

> The price will rise at the full release. Buying during Early Access is the cheaper way
> in and always will be.

**How are you planning on involving the Community in your development process?**

> This game is developed in the open: the source, the design notes and the reasoning
> behind every decision are public, and so is the list of what is being worked on. Reports
> from the water — *the brig does not say when she is following me*, *the sails are drawn
> too low* — have each become a change within a day. That is how it will keep working.

**There is a first set of capsules**, made by `dist/capsules.py` out of a picture the game
took of itself with its own furniture turned off, set in the game's own face with the
game's own accent. It is deliberately a poster rather than a screenshot: cropped hard
around the rig, a stop down, the contrast up, and the name in VT323 with the amber full
stop the console and the website both use.

**Whether that is good enough is a judgement, not a measurement.** Put it beside the
capsules on any Steam search page and it will be obvious within a second. If it reads
weakly, the answer is an illustrator and a few hundred dollars, and the script's job is
then to be deleted rather than defended — but a shop page that looks like the game is
worth more than a shop page that looks expensive, and this one at least looks like the
game.

The **library logo**, which sits over the hero as transparent artwork, is the one piece
with no honest generated answer. That one is a pen.

### Screenshots, in the order they should sit on the page

1. **The gale** — forty-six knots, three and a half metres of sea, reefed and heeled.
   This is the one that says the physics is real.
2. **The chart on the table** — dividers, parallel rule, the coast, the track. This is
   the one that says the navigation is real.
3. **The brig and the signal halyard** — a square-rigger astern, a flag up, and her
   answer. This is the one nobody else has.
4. **The ink sea** — the drawn picture, which is the thing people will screenshot for us.
5. **The platform at dusk** — the lamp lit, the casks stacked, coming home.
6. **At the helm** — the deck, the sail bags, the console: the boat as a place.

**`dist/store.sh` takes all six**, the way `site/shoot.sh` takes the page's: the game is
told to start, sail long enough for the watch to have trimmed her, photograph itself and
quit. It checks each one for the two failures that look like success — a black frame, and
a frame that is the wrong shape for the shop — and it stages them against a save of its
own, so a screenshot run cannot touch the player's platform.

### The trailer

**The game cannot film itself at speed.** The screenshot dials can take one frame at a
time, but past about twelve frames a second the capture is handed a picture that has not
been drawn into it yet and every frame comes back black — and it comes back black
*looking like success*: the right count of files, the right names, eleven kilobytes of
nothing each. `dist/trailer.sh` therefore records at twelve, checks brightness as well as
count, retries a dead scene once, and lets ffmpeg interpolate up to twenty-four. That
produces a cut. It does not produce footage.

So the trailer is shot the other way round: **something else films the screen** — a
screen recorder, sixty real frames a second, with the sound — and the game puts on the
show on its own. `SAIL_REEL=1` runs a timetable (`crates/sail-app/src/reel.rs`), seventeen
beats over a hundred and two seconds, with nothing to press:

| From | To | The shot |
|---|---|---|
| 0 s | 9 s | Astern of her, force 4, morning — the shot that says what the game is |
| 9 s | 20 s | All the way round her: rig, heel, wake, hull |
| 21 s | 31 s | The helmsman's seat — the deck as a place |
| 31 s | 43 s | The chart spread on the table, the console lit |
| 44 s | 57 s | Force 8 on a built sea, from the seat she was just sailing quietly in |
| 57 s | 68 s | The drawn sea: the same water, told differently |
| 69 s | 78 s | Evening, the platform fine on the bow, its lamp lighting itself |
| 78 s | 88 s | K at the masthead, and the brig's pendant answering |
| 88 s | 102 s | Night: a moon, a path under it, the chart in the corner under its lamp |

Each scene is long enough to take six seconds out of the middle of, away from the moment
the weather jumps — which is a test (`the_running_order`) rather than a hope. Record it,
then cut the nine shots down to the sixty seconds Steam wants.

**It is not a cutscene.** Every beat writes the same resource the settings panel writes;
anything the reel can show, a player can do.

### And the short one — `SAIL_REEL=short`

The passage reel is for somebody deciding whether to buy it: it has the instruments in it,
because they are half the game. **This one is for somebody deciding whether to look at
it**, and what decides that is water, light and cloth. No panels, no mission, no text —
ninety-three seconds of a boat sailing, to be cut down to about fifty-five.

| From | To | The shot |
|---|---|---|
| 0 s | 10 s | **Sunrise.** 04:45 to 06:09 with the sky's clock at five hundred and twenty. The sun breaks the horizon about five seconds in |
| 10 s | 18 s | **First light, close-hauled.** The cockpit: the deck, the heel, low sun through the genoa |
| 18 s | 30 s | **Bearing away.** Fifty degrees to a hundred and thirty-five, the camera walking round her the other way, the genoa rolling away as she goes |
| 30 s | 42 s | **The kite.** The bow clear, the gennaker up off the sprit, full and drawing by the end |
| 42 s | 54 s | **And in again** — twelve seconds before the breeze arrives, which is when a kite actually comes down |
| 54 s | 66 s | **It comes on to blow.** Force 7, two slabs in the main, the genoa unrolling into it, seen from aft where the wake tilts |
| 66 s | 76 s | **Sunset**, the clock back up to five hundred and twenty; the sun on the horizon at about seventy-one |
| 76 s | 85 s | **Moonrise**, the camera coming round to the opposite bearing — which is where a full moon always is |
| 85 s | 93 s | **Night under the moon**, its path on the water |

Three numbers shape it and all three were measured rather than chosen:

* **A sail change is twenty-four seconds.** The furling drum runs at 0.085 a second and a
  change is two full turns of it — roll the old one away, swap at the bow, set the new
  one. The first cut of this reel ordered the gennaker ten seconds before it wanted it and
  filmed a boat with a bare forestay. Every order is now given a shot early, and the
  rolling away is part of the shot before.
* **The kite comes in before the wind comes up.** A gennaker in force 7 is over its cloth,
  and cloth over its number has about five seconds left; the twelve it takes to roll away
  would have blown it out. Which is also how it works at sea.
* **Sunrise here is 05:29 and sunset 18:31** — fifty-seven north, five degrees of
  declination, off the same two sines the sky itself uses. The moonrise is free: a full
  moon is at the antisolar point, so it comes up as the sun goes down and the camera only
  has to turn round.

`SAIL_REEL_AFTER=30` holds everything back half a minute, so there is time to find the
window and get the mouse out of the frame before the first beat fires.

There is **no brig in this one** and nothing to signal to — it is the boat and the sea.

## System requirements

**Minimum**

* OS: Windows 10 64-bit / macOS 12 / Ubuntu 22.04
* Processor: four cores
* Memory: 8 GB RAM
* Graphics: anything with Vulkan, Metal or DirectX 12 — integrated included
* Storage: 1 GB
* Notes: a keyboard. A gamepad works; a mouse is wanted for the chart table.

**Recommended**

* Processor: eight cores — the sea's spectrum is built across all of them at launch
* Memory: 16 GB RAM
* Graphics: a discrete card, for the full-resolution picture at sixty frames

## Before the page goes live

* The store page must be approved and visible **at least two weeks** before release, and
  there is a **thirty-day wait** from paying the Steam Direct fee to being allowed to
  release at all. Both clocks start when you start them.
* Build uploaded through SteamPipe from CI, on a tag, beside the GitHub release that
  already happens.
* macOS depot signed and notarised, or the first thing a Mac owner sees is Gatekeeper.
* A privacy note for the multiplayer: what crosses the wire is a name, a position and a
  boat's state; nothing is stored on the relay.
